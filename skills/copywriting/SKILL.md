# Copywriting

Copy exists to move one reader to one action. These rules keep it specific,
scannable, and honest.

## Rules

### MUST
- Put the benefit before the feature. Say what the reader gets, then how the product does it.
- Write every call to action as a clear verb plus the outcome the reader gets. "Start your free trial" beats "Submit".
- Write to one buyer. Pick a single reader and address them directly, not "teams", "users", or "everyone".
- Make headlines specific. Name the outcome, the number, the audience, or the mechanism. Never a vague promise.
- Give each section one idea. If a section argues two things, split it.
- Make the above-the-fold section sell on its own. A reader who never scrolls should still know what it is, who it is for, and why they should act.
- Make the body support the headline. Every section below the headline supplies evidence, detail, or handling of an objection for the claim the headline makes.

### SHOULD
- Break up blocks of text. Short paragraphs, subheads, and white space.
- Make copy scannable. A reader skimming headlines, bold leads, and bullets should get the whole pitch.
- Write for lazy readers. Assume no attention, no patience, and no prior context.
- Keep it punchy. Cut padding, qualifiers, and warm-up sentences.
- Use one to three key bullets per section. More than three dilutes the point.

### NEVER
- Include unnecessary information. If a line does not move the reader toward the action, cut it.
- Open with a generic opener. No "In today's fast-paced world", no "Welcome to", no throat-clearing.
- Fabricate claims. No invented numbers, testimonials, customers, awards, or results. If there is no source, ask for one or leave it out.
- Use two-beat antithesis. No "It's not X. It's Y." or "Not a tool. A teammate." State the point directly.
- Use em dashes.
- Use aphorism formulas. No manufactured wisdom such as "The future isn't coming. It's here." or "Less, but better." End on a concrete point or a call to action.

## How to apply

1. Identify the one buyer and the one action before writing anything. If either is unclear, ask.
2. Write the headline first, as a specific claim. Then write the above-the-fold section so it sells alone: what it is, who it is for, why act now.
3. Outline sections, one idea each, and order them so each supports the headline claim.
4. Draft each section benefit-first, with at most three bullets.
5. Write each CTA as verb plus outcome.
6. Cut: unnecessary info, generic openers, two-beat antithesis, em dashes, aphorism formulas, and any claim without a source.
7. Skim test: read only the headline, subheads, bold leads, and bullets. If the pitch does not come through, restructure.
8. For AI-sounding prose problems beyond these rules, apply the `no-ai-slop` skill.

## Examples

Headline, bad:

```text
A better way to manage your projects.
```

Headline, good:

```text
Ship client projects on time without weekly status meetings.
```

CTA, bad:

```text
Learn more
```

CTA, good:

```text
Get your first invoice out today
```

Benefit before feature, bad:

```text
Built-in OCR with 99.2% accuracy on receipts.
```

Benefit before feature, good:

```text
Stop typing receipts by hand. Snap a photo and the numbers land in your books, with 99.2% OCR accuracy.
```

Two-beat antithesis, bad:

```text
This isn't a to-do app. It's a second brain.
```

Two-beat antithesis, good:

```text
Everything you capture is searchable and linked, so nothing you noted gets lost.
```

## Rationale

Readers of marketing copy are skimming, skeptical, and one click from leaving.
Specific headlines and benefit-first framing earn the next second of
attention. One idea per section and a scannable layout let a skimmer absorb
the pitch. Verb-plus-outcome CTAs tell the reader exactly what happens next.
The banned patterns are the fingerprints of generic or AI-generated copy, and
fabricated claims destroy trust the moment a reader checks.
